# Subgroup Proofs

Four worked problems following the scheme from [[substructures]]:
(i) closure (Abgeschlossenheit) of the operation, (ii) closure of taking inverses.

## 1. $U = \{1, -1\}$ in $(\mathbb{Q}^*, \cdot, 1)$, $\mathbb{Q}^* = \mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}$

**(i)** $(\pm 1) \cdot (\pm 1) \in \{1, -1\} = U$ ✓
**(ii)** $1^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{1} = 1 \in U$ and $(-1)^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{-1} = -1 \in U$ ✓

$\Rightarrow$ $U$ is a subgroup (Untergruppe). $\square$

## 2. The even integers $U = \{2z \mid z \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ in $(\mathbb{Z}, +, 0)$

**(i)** For $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$: $\;2a + 2b = 2(a+b) \in U$ ✓
**(ii)** For $2a \in U$: $\;-2a = 2(-a) \in U$ ✓

$\Rightarrow$ $U$ is a subgroup. $\square$

Note the **additive notation** (additive Schreibweise): the inverse is $-x$, not $x^{-1}$.

## 3. The alternating group $A_n = \{\pi \in S_n \mid \operatorname{sgn}(\pi) = 1\}$ in $S_n$

**(i) Closure.** Let $\pi, \sigma \in A_n$, i.e.
$\operatorname{sgn}(\pi) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) = 1$. Then
$$\operatorname{sgn}(\pi \sigma) = \operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) = 1 \cdot 1 = 1
\;\Rightarrow\; \pi\sigma \in A_n \quad ✓$$
**(ii) Inverses.** For $\pi \in A_n$, $\operatorname{sgn}(\pi^{-1}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\pi) = 1$,
so $\pi^{-1} \in A_n$ ✓

$\square$

The proof uses that $\operatorname{sgn}$ is **multiplicative**, i.e. a
[[homomorphism|homomorphism]] $S_n \to \{\pm 1\}$. $A_n$ is thus exactly the kernel of
this homomorphism.

## 4. Rotations of the equilateral triangle

The permutations (Permutationen) of degree 3 form
$$S_3 = \{\mathrm{id}, \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \tau_1, \tau_2, \tau_3\}$$
with the rotations (Drehungen) $\mathrm{id} = (1)(2)(3)$, $\sigma_1 = (1\,2\,3)$,
$\sigma_2 = (1\,3\,2)$ and the three reflections (Spiegelungen) $\tau_i$.

Consider the subset of rotations $R = \{\mathrm{id}, \sigma_1, \sigma_2\}$.

**(i) Closure** — via the operation table (Verknüpfungstafel):

| $\circ$ | $\mathrm{id}$ | $\sigma_1$ | $\sigma_2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{id}$ | $\mathrm{id}$ | $\sigma_1$ | $\sigma_2$ |
| $\sigma_1$ | $\sigma_1$ | $\sigma_2$ | $\mathrm{id}$ |
| $\sigma_2$ | $\sigma_2$ | $\mathrm{id}$ | $\sigma_1$ |

All entries lie in $R$ ✓

**(ii) Inverses.** $\mathrm{id}^{-1} = \mathrm{id}$, $\sigma_1^{-1} = \sigma_2$,
$\sigma_2^{-1} = \sigma_1$ — all in $R$ ✓

$\Rightarrow$ $R$ is a subgroup of $S_3$. $\square$

The table also shows: $R$ is **cyclic**, generated by $\sigma_1$ with
$\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2$, $\sigma_1^3 = \mathrm{id}$.

## Contradictions

**p. 13: the name $D_3$.** The source calls the set of rotations
$\{\mathrm{id}, \sigma_1, \sigma_2\}$ "$D_3$". This is misleading:

> [!inferred] not from the summaries
> $D_3$ usually denotes the **dihedral group** of the triangle, i.e. all symmetries — 3
> rotations *and* 3 reflections, 6 elements in total, which here would be all of $S_3$.
> The 3-element rotation group considered in the source is the cyclic group $C_3$, which
> at the same time is the alternating group $A_3$. Which notation the module actually
> uses remains to be clarified.

The neutral name $R$ was therefore used above.

## What it shows

- For groups, **only two** conditions are needed; $e \in U$ follows automatically —
  see [[substructures]].
- For small, finite sets, the **operation table** is the fastest way to show closure
  (point 4). For infinite sets one must compute in general (points 1–3).
- Point 3 is the pattern "preimage of a homomorphism is a subgroup" — noticeably more
  elegant than computing with individual permutations.
- Counterpart: [[submonoid-proofs]].
